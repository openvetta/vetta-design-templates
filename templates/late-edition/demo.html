The order

From tonight the east quay takes mail, ice, and this paper. Passengers stay west until the forty-two lamps are named.

The harbor master posted the order at noon and nailed a second copy to the east shed, where the paint is still new. From tonight the quay takes boats again, but only those carrying mail, ice, and this paper.

Passengers remain on the west side until every new lamp has been counted, named, and written into the cloth book. The book stays in the shed. It does not travel.

A third copy stands in the window of the stair, facing the people already waiting. They can read it. They cannot yet cross. The shed clock is ten minutes fast, and the master has left it so.

The first sack will be logged when it is on the scale, not when the clock says nine. Until then the east side is a place for reading the order, and for nothing else.

Children have been turned back twice already. The master wrote their names in the margin and told them the west stair is the place to wait. The margin is full. The stair is not. Someone has brought a chair. It faces the window copy, not the water.

The count is deliberately plain. Forty-two lamps, one keeper, no deputies. The city paid for the piles in March and left the lighting to the harbor, which has asked for nothing else.

“We would rather open late than open dim,” the master said, and folded the order once along a crease that has already gone soft. The keeper begins at dusk and reads the numbers aloud.

The lamps run from the shed out to the last pile. Names already taken include East Shed, First Pile, and The Bend. The furthest lamp is still “unnamed,” in pencil.

The keeper will not light a pencil. When the last name is written in ink, the quay opens, and not a minute before. A spare book sits under the kettle, still blank, in case the first one fills. The ink is the same black as this paper. The harbor buys it by the bottle.

On the quay the work has already turned ordinary: coils of line, a scale, a kettle left from the afternoon shift. The night market does not return this week, whatever the west-side posters still promise.

What returns is the crossing, and the habit of waiting for the first sack, due at nine. Ice for it sits under sailcloth in the low shed, weighed at the corners with chain.

The market’s fish stays west until the book is finished. No one has argued. The kettle is for the keeper, not for sale. The posters come down in the morning, and the night market waits for a later edition than this one.

Tonight the quay is a crossing, a scale, and a book. That is the whole of the news. The rest of the city can read it here, and wait its turn on the west side.

02 — Columns

Rules do the dividing

A thick rule and a thin rule hold the nameplate. One-pixel rules separate the columns, the index, and the notices. Nothing is a card.

03 — Type

Serif for the sheet, grotesque for the folio

The nameplate is tracked capitals. The headline is sentence case and heavier than the body. The deck is italic. Labels stay small.

04 — Order

An underline, not a stamp

“Read the order” is a line of type with a target tall enough to hit. Hover changes its color. It does not grow a shape.
